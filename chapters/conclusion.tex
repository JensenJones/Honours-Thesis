\chapter{Conclusion}

This work extended the simulation to support GPU-backed field data and custom pulse
profiles. By adding custom-defined functions, users are now able to define their own pulse shapes and spatially 
dependent fields without changing any code. The research then showed that GPU acceleration 
can greatly speed up simulation, and therefore research. GPU acceleration was seen to have greater 
benefit as the workload got larger, through increasing grid size or number of channels. For small grids, 
the time taken to launch GPU operations can make the GPU slower than the CPU.
For larger grids and more channels, the GPU was substantially faster, reaching roughly 19$\times$ improvements.

Some interesting findings came from looking further into the speed of GPU computation. Breaking the
runtime into its linear and nonlinear parts revealed that the GPU does not help every part of the
simulation equally. The FFT-heavy linear step gained far more than the nonlinear step, which spends
much of its time reading and writing the field data rather than computing. Investigating single
precision raised a similar tension. Dropping to 32-bit values on the GPU made the simulation roughly 2.4 times
faster than 64-bit, which was promising given how heavily many GPUs penalise double precision, but it came with
a steady loss of energy that was eventually traced to round-off in the single-precision FFTs. With
Kerr effects enabled, the error grew roughly quadratically with step count. Single precision is
therefore useful for quick exploratory runs, but not yet trustworthy for long simulations, and
finding a way to keep its speed without its error is one of the more promising directions this work
leaves open. On the software side, building \texttt{FieldData} around a \texttt{std::variant} proved
to be a good fit. It let the CPU and GPU share the same propagation code, caught missing backing
implementations at compile time, and leaves room for future backings to be added without touching
the rest of the simulation.

The outcome of this research is that the SBS simulation is now capable of utilising the previously
unrealised computational power of the GPU.
